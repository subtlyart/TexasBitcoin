Bitcoin, the Texas way

The Blocksize War

Why Bitcoin blocks are small on purpose — and how a two-year brawl over a single setting decided what Bitcoin would be. Told through Dale, a longhorn who refuses to panic.

Nobody could agree how big a Bitcoin block should be. So Texas settled it: can't make the block bigger? Grow a bigger kernel.

They called it a blocksize war. We call it harvest season. 🌽

For two years Bitcoiners nearly tore the whole thing apart over one number: how many megabytes of transactions fit in a block. It sounds like a plumbing argument. It was really a fight over what Bitcoin is for — and the side that won is the reason Dale can still run a full node from a shed in Rockdale.

01 / THE SETTINGWhy there's a limit at all

A Bitcoin block is a batch of transactions added to the chain about every ten minutes. In 2010, Satoshi Nakamoto quietly capped each block at roughly 1 megabyte — a spam-and-denial-of-service guard, meant to stop anyone from stuffing the chain with junk nobody could afford to store.

That cap also set a ceiling on throughput: somewhere around 3–7 transactions per second, forever, no matter how many people showed up. For years it didn't matter. By 2015, with blocks filling up and fees climbing, it mattered a lot.

The question underneath: should Bitcoin stay small enough that an ordinary person can verify it, or grow big enough to be everyone's daily payment rail?

02 / THE TWO SIDESBig blocks vs. small blocks

Big-blockers wanted to raise the cap — to 2MB, 8MB, eventually gigabytes. More space per block means more transactions and cheaper fees. Their vision was Bitcoin as cash for the planet.

Small-blockers wanted the cap to stay low. Their logic: if blocks get huge, only data centers can afford to store and verify the chain, and ordinary people stop running their own full nodes. Lose the hobbyist nodes, they argued, and you lose the decentralization that made Bitcoin worth anything. Scaling should happen in layers on top of the chain, not by inflating the chain itself.

Both sides believed the other was going to destroy Bitcoin. Neither was entirely wrong about the stakes.

03 / THE WEAPONSForks, flags, and a street fight over code

You can't vote on Bitcoin. You change it by getting people to run different software — so each camp shipped its own. Big-blockers launched Bitcoin XT, then Bitcoin Classic, then Bitcoin Unlimited. Small-blockers rallied behind the existing software and a soft-fork upgrade called SegWit.

In 2017 miners and companies cut a backroom deal, the New York Agreement (also "SegWit2x"): activate SegWit now, then double the block size later. Users who distrusted the deal threatened a UASF — a user-activated soft fork (BIP148) that would orphan any block not signaling SegWit. It was brinkmanship with real money on the line.

04 / THE RESOLUTIONHow it actually ended

SegWit activated in August 2017 as a soft fork. It raised effective capacity (blocks now measured in "weight," up to ~4 million units) and fixed a bug that made the Lightning Network — fast, cheap payments layered on top — practical.

The big-blockers who wouldn't accept it split off on August 1, 2017 and created Bitcoin Cash, with 8MB blocks. The promised "2x" hard fork was called off in November 2017 for lack of support. Small blocks held.

Bitcoin kept the chain small and pushed scale into layers above it. Which is exactly why, nine years on, a longhorn with a mining shed can still verify the entire network himself — no permission, no data center.

Plain-English glossary

Block
A bundle of transactions added to the chain roughly every ten minutes.
Block size cap
The limit on how much data fits in one block — the ~1MB rule the whole war was about.
Full node
Software that independently stores and checks the entire blockchain. Cheap full nodes = decentralization. Keeping them cheap was the small-blockers' core goal.
Soft fork
A backward-compatible upgrade (like SegWit). Old software still works.
Hard fork
An incompatible change that splits the network into two chains — how Bitcoin Cash was born.
SegWit
"Segregated Witness," the 2017 soft fork that raised capacity and unlocked the Lightning Network.
Lightning Network
A layer built on top of Bitcoin for fast, cheap payments — the small-blockers' answer to scaling.
UASF
User-Activated Soft Fork: node operators forcing an upgrade without waiting for miners to agree.

The one thing to walk away with

"Y'all are gonna have to wait" isn't a bug in Bitcoin — it's the whole design philosophy that won. Small blocks keep the network cheap enough for anyone to verify, and the real scaling moved to layers on top. The limit is the feature. Dale knew. Dale always knew.
