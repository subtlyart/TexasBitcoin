Bitcoin, the Texas way

Fire to Sats

Texas burns off enough natural gas to make the night sky glow from orbit. Some of that fire now mines Bitcoin instead. Here's how waste became hashrate — and where the honest catch is.

Texas flared enough gas to light up satellite photos. Now some of it mines Bitcoin. We were gonna burn it anyway.

Fire-to-sats. The most Texan sentence ever spoken. 🔥→₿

When you drill for oil, natural gas comes up with it — whether you want it or not. Out in the Permian Basin there's often no pipeline to carry that gas anywhere, so producers do the cheapest legal thing: they set it on fire. That's a flare. Billions of cubic feet of it, year after year, burning for nothing. Bitcoin miners looked at all that wasted fire and saw a power plant.

01 / THE WASTEWhy gas gets burned in the first place

Oil is the money; the gas that rises with it is "associated gas." Where there's no pipeline or it's not worth building one, that gas is stranded. Producers either flare it (burn it off) or, worse, vent it (release it straight into the air as methane).

Texas has flared and vented enormous volumes — at peaks, enough to rank among the largest flaring regions on Earth, bright enough to show up in nighttime satellite imagery. It's wasted energy and wasted money, every single day.

A flare is a dollar bill on fire. Bitcoin mining is the one buyer that can show up anywhere, instantly, and take all of it.

02 / THE FIXPut a generator and a miner on the wellhead

The move is simple to describe: haul a generator and a shipping container full of mining computers out to the well. Feed the stranded gas into the generator, make electricity on-site, and point that electricity at Bitcoin mining — which is just computers racing to earn the next block reward.

Because a miner is a buyer that doesn't need a pipeline, a grid connection, or a customer down the road, it can plug into gas that had no other market at all. Companies like Crusoe, Giga, and others built whole businesses doing exactly this.

03 / THE EMISSIONS ANGLEWhy a generator can beat a flare

Here's the part people fight about. A flare doesn't burn perfectly — some gas escapes as methane, which traps heat roughly 80× as hard as CO₂ over 20 years. A well-run engine combusts the gas more completely than an open flare, so advocates argue it can cut the methane that would otherwise slip out, and it beats venting by a mile.

That's the strongest version of the case: not that it's clean, but that it's cleaner than the alternative that was already happening.

Plain-English glossary

Flaring
Burning off unwanted natural gas at the well. Visible as a flame on a stack.
Venting
Releasing the gas unburned — worse, because raw methane is a potent greenhouse gas.
Associated gas
Natural gas that comes up alongside oil, often with no pipeline to sell it.
Stranded energy
Power with no market where it's produced. Mining's specialty — it's a buyer anywhere.
Hashrate
How much computing a miner throws at finding the next block. More power, more hashrate.
Permian Basin
The West Texas / New Mexico oil region where much of this flaring — and now mining — happens.

The honest catch

This isn't green energy. It's still burning a fossil fuel, and critics make two real points: paying producers for stranded gas could make marginal drilling more attractive, and the climate benefit depends entirely on comparing against gas that would truly have been flared or vented anyway. The win is "better than setting it on fire for nothing" — not "zero emissions." bitscorn tells the joke; we won't oversell the fire.

The one thing to walk away with

Bitcoin mining is a buyer of last resort for energy that had no other buyer. In Texas that means stranded well-gas that was already on fire. Dale's floating on money that used to be smoke — and the strongest case for it is modest and honest: better than the flare, not better than clean.
