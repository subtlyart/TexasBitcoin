Bitcoin, the Texas way

Not Your Grid

Texas runs its own power grid on purpose — so it answers to nobody. It's why Bitcoin miners moved here, why they sometimes get paid to switch off, and why one winter it nearly all went dark.

Grid operator: "It's 108°, can you power down the miner?"
Dale: "I was already not doing anything."
Operator: "Perfect. We'll pay you for it."

Not your grid, not your watts. 🏴 That's demand response, and in Texas it's a revenue stream.

Almost the whole country is wired into two giant power grids that cross state lines. Texas looked at that and said: no thanks. It runs its own grid, ERCOT, kept deliberately separate from the rest — and that one stubborn choice shapes everything about why Bitcoin mining boomed here.

01 / THE SETUPA grid that answers to nobody

The US has three grids: the Eastern Interconnection, the Western Interconnection, and Texas. ERCOT — the Electric Reliability Council of Texas — runs the power for most of the state and pointedly does not connect across state lines.

Why go it alone? Because selling electricity across state lines triggers federal oversight. By keeping its grid inside Texas, the state sidesteps a big chunk of federal regulation and keeps the rules to itself. It's independence as infrastructure — the most Texan thing imaginable, poured into concrete and copper.

Miners love it: light-touch rules, cheap land, and a grid desperate for big flexible customers it can lean on when things get tight.

02 / THE CATCHFebruary 2021, when the lights went out

Independence cuts both ways. In Winter Storm Uri (February 2021), generators froze, demand spiked, and because Texas is walled off from its neighbors, it couldn't import much power to fill the gap. Millions lost electricity for days in lethal cold. People died.

That's the honest shadow over the whole "we don't need anybody" story: the same isolation that keeps federal regulators out also keeps emergency help out. bitscorn jokes about floating through a heat wave — but the grid's fragility is real, and it's part of the picture.

03 / THE TWISTGetting paid to turn off

Here's where miners become useful instead of just hungry. A Bitcoin miner is a flexible load — it can power down in seconds without ruining anything. So when the grid is strained and every megawatt counts, miners shut off and sell their reserved power back. That's demand response.

During extreme demand, the payments for powering down can exceed what the miner would have earned mining. Big operators have taken millions in such credits during peak events. Dale gets compensated to float and do nothing, which is both a punchline and an actual market product.

Plain-English glossary

ERCOT
Electric Reliability Council of Texas — the operator of the state's standalone grid.
Interconnection
A region of synchronized grid. The US has three; Texas is its own.
Flexible / controllable load
A big electricity user that can ramp down on demand. Miners are the textbook example.
Demand response
Paying consumers to use less power when the grid is stressed, instead of only paying generators to make more.
Curtailment
Deliberately cutting a load's power draw — voluntarily, for a payment, or by order.
Winter Storm Uri
The Feb 2021 freeze that triggered catastrophic Texas blackouts and exposed the grid's isolation.

The honest catch

Paying miners to switch off is genuinely useful grid behavior — but it's contested. Critics argue miners profit handsomely from scarcity they didn't create, and that subsidizing flexible demand is a strange use of public markets. Defenders counter that a large, truly interruptible load makes the grid more stable and helps fund new generation. Both can be true. And none of it undoes the lesson of Uri: a grid that can't call for help is a grid that can fail alone.

The one thing to walk away with

Texas built its own grid to stay free of federal rules — which drew the miners, created the "paid to power down" market, and left the state to face its worst storm alone. Dale floating while the grid pays him to do nothing is funny because it's real. The independence is the whole story: the upside and the risk, same coin.
